CHAPTER 01

Accounts, identity and guardrails

Who may do what in AWS, and the layers that cap it: the organization, the role, and the request itself.

1.1What is a Service Control Policy (SCP)?

AWS Organizations groups many AWS accounts under one management account. An SCP is a guardrail the organization attaches to an account, or to a group of accounts (an organizational unit, OU). It sets the maximum permissions anyone in that account can have.

  • It never grants anything. A request succeeds only if the SCP allows it and the caller's own IAM permissions allow it.
  • An explicit Deny in an SCP beats everything, even an administrator role or the account's root user.
  • Only the organization's admins can change SCPs, from the management account.
  • Typical uses: only approved regions; no leaving the organization; no turning off CloudTrail; nothing that exposes workloads to the internet (Elastic IPs, internet gateways, public IPs) outside the networking team's control.
In our account

A dry run of ec2:AllocateAddress returned "explicit deny in a service control policy", so our admin role can't create Elastic IPs. We weren't at the quota: 2 of 5 used.

1.2What is IAM Identity Center?

IAM Identity Center, formerly "AWS SSO", is AWS's single sign-on for people who work across many accounts. It's run centrally, usually from the organization's management account.

  • Users: stored in Identity Center's own directory or, more commonly, synced from the company identity provider (Entra, Okta, Google Workspace) over SAML and SCIM. People sign in with their normal company login and MFA.
  • Permission sets: bundles of IAM policies such as "Workload-Admin" or "ReadOnly". Admins assign a person or group a permission set in specific accounts.
  • Roles: each assignment becomes an IAM role in that account, named AWSReservedSSO_<permission set>_<id>.
  • Access portal: a page listing the accounts and roles you may use, to open the console or get CLI credentials.
  • CLI credentials are temporary. The session that renews them lasts a fixed time, often 8 to 12 hours, which is why aws login expires and asks for a browser sign-in again. No long-lived access keys exist.

How it fits with SCPs: Identity Center decides who may become which role in which account. SCPs then cap what any role in that account can do. So even an "admin" permission set can't allocate an Elastic IP where an SCP denies it.

Not the same as an access proxy. Identity Center signs people in to AWS. A proxy such as Cloudflare Access gates an app's web traffic (5.1). Both can use the same company identity provider underneath.

In our account

We sign in through Identity Center with the Workload-Admin-PS permission set, in region il-central-1.

In Crucible

Identity Center can also act as a SAML identity provider for applications. Crucible speaks OIDC, so the pattern would be Crucible → Cognito → Identity Center (SAML). Colleagues would then sign in to Crucible with the same company login they use for AWS.

1.3What does "the node role is trusted into the lab account" mean?

An IAM role has two halves:

  • Its permission policies: what the role may do.
  • Its trust policy: who may become it, by calling sts:AssumeRole.

The lab role's trust policy names one identity: the IAM role of Crucible's EC2 node. So only Crucible's server can assume it. AWS then returns temporary lab credentials that last an hour.

Why "in one account it's the same rule"

For one role to assume another, AWS checks two things: does the caller's own policy allow sts:AssumeRole on the target, and does the target's trust policy accept the caller?

  • Across two accounts, AWS always requires both.
  • Within one account, a trust policy that names the caller's role directly can be enough by itself.
  • Crucible's lab role is written the other way: its trust names the whole account, plus a condition that the caller must be the node role's ARN. With that form, the node role's own policy must allow the call too.

So both halves are needed whether the lab account is separate or the same one, and the same Terraform works for both.

1.4Is Crucible's lab account literally a second AWS account? Does labs-init create one?

It's meant to be separate, but it doesn't have to be, and labs-init doesn't create one. It runs the labs Terraform stack in whatever account your current credentials point at.

  • Recommended: a separate account, created by you first (AWS Organizations → Add account). Then run crucible aws labs-init with credentials for it and --crucible-account <main account id>. Why it's better:
    • Containment: what a lab or trainee does stays away from Crucible's own servers, buckets and Cognito.
    • Cost: the billing export, the AWS Budget and the cost alerts cover only lab spend.
    • Cleanup: the reaper can treat everything tagged crucible:lab-id in that account as lab-owned.
  • One account also works. Leave out --crucible-account, and the labs stack trusts the node in its own account. One thing to know: the labs stack turns on S3's account-wide public-access block, so check that nothing else there needs public buckets.
In our account

The organization's SCPs would also apply to labs. Ideally labs get a sandbox account without the networking restrictions, since labs create EC2 and S3 resources freely.

1.5What is a permission boundary? Does it limit what Terraform can do?

A permission boundary is an IAM ceiling attached to a role. A request succeeds only if both the role's own policy and the boundary allow it, and an explicit Deny in either wins (see the diagram in 1.1).

Yes, it limits Terraform, inside AWS rather than in the module. Terraform can declare anything. But every apply, destroy, workspace terminal and sweep acts with a lab-role session, so every AWS call is checked. A module that declares something outside the limits passes plan, then fails at apply with AccessDenied.

1.6How does IAM stop a lab from touching another lab's resources?

Three pieces work together: a tag fixed on the credentials, one policy that refers to it, and tags forced into every resource.

  1. Session tags. Crucible assumes the lab role with the session tag crucible:lab-id=<id>, and the trust policy requires that tag. The trainee holds the resulting one-hour credentials but can't assume the role again, so they can't swap the id.
  2. One policy for every lab. The policy refers to ${aws:PrincipalTag/crucible:lab-id}. AWS fills that in with the caller's own lab id on every request, so each lab only ever matches itself.
  3. Creates must carry the tag. RunInstances, CreateVolume and CreateSecurityGroup are allowed only if the request tags the resource with your own lab id (aws:RequestTag). CreateTags works only as part of a create (ec2:CreateAction), so you can't create first and tag later. S3 buckets are limited by name instead (crucible-lab-<id>…).
  4. Tags can't be removed or changed. Changing crucible:* tags after creation is denied, and so is "delete all tags", which carries no key list.
  5. Only your own resources. Stopping, changing or deleting requires the resource to carry your lab tag (aws:ResourceTag).
  6. Only your own security group. EC2 checks every security group and network interface in a launch as a resource of its own. Ones tagged for another lab, or untagged (like the VPC's shared default group), are denied.

Terraform's side: Crucible writes the provider block itself, with default_tags on every resource, and lint refuses a provider block in the author's module.

In Crucible

These rules are pinned by deploy/aws/labs/labs.tftest.hcl, a Terraform test with a mock AWS provider. It checks that the policy documents say the right thing. Only a real account can show that AWS enforces them, so the runbook has by-hand checks.

1.7What is CloudTrail?

AWS's audit log. Every API call in the account is recorded:

  • who made it, what it was, when, and from which IP;
  • whether it was allowed;
  • whether it came from a console click, a CLI command or a role, such as a lab's Terraform creating an instance.

The last 90 days are searchable by default. Organizations usually also copy everything to a central, tamper-proof bucket, and an SCP stops anyone turning it off.

1.8What is IMDS?

The EC2 Instance Metadata Service, at http://169.254.169.254 from inside any EC2 instance. Among other things it hands out the instance's IAM role credentials.

That makes it a target: a pod on the node that reaches it gets the node's credentials.

In Crucible

On Crucible's node, those credentials can assume the lab, ops and plan roles. So:

  • lab pods' NetworkPolicy blocks egress to 169.254.169.254;
  • pods set AWS_EC2_METADATA_DISABLED;
  • an init container makes the pod wait until the block is in place.

cluster-check proves the block with a stand-in metadata server on kind.

1.9What does "a shell through SSM" mean?

SSM is AWS Systems Manager; the shell comes from its Session Manager feature.

  1. The agent: an SSM agent on the instance keeps an outbound HTTPS connection to the SSM service. The instance's role needs the AmazonSSMManagedInstanceCore policy.
  2. Opening a shell: you run aws ssm start-session --target <instance-id>, with the Session Manager plugin installed. AWS relays a terminal over that connection.
  3. Why it's better than SSH:
    • no open SSH port and no keys to manage;
    • access is controlled by IAM (ssm:StartSession);
    • sessions can be recorded.

Port forwarding works the same way and reaches a private app from your laptop without a VPN:

aws ssm start-session --target <node-instance-id> \
  --document-name AWS-StartPortForwardingSession \
  --parameters 'portNumber=80,localPortNumber=8080'
# then open http://localhost:8080

That's for admins and testing, not for handing to trainees.

CHAPTER 02

VPC networking

How a private network inside AWS is built, which doors lead in and out, and how our organization's network is laid out.

2.1What is a VPC?

A Virtual Private Cloud is your own private network inside AWS, in one region. It's made of:

  • An IP range in CIDR notation, e.g. 10.2.128.0/22: 1,024 private addresses that mean nothing outside your network.
  • Subnets: slices of that range, each in one data center (availability zone, AZ).
  • Route tables: they decide where each subnet's traffic goes.
  • Gateways:
    • an internet gateway is the door to the internet;
    • a NAT gateway lets traffic out but nothing in;
    • a VPC endpoint is a private path to one service.
  • Security groups: the firewall on each network interface.

Is a "private subnet" in AWS? Yes, subnets are part of the VPC. "Private" means its route table has no route to the internet gateway and its servers have no public IPs.

2.2How is our organization's network built?

The central networking team gives each account a standard spoke VPC, built by a stack named SPOKE-Networking-…. Ours spans two availability zones, with three tiers of subnet.

  • the firewall to allow the node's outbound hosts.

2.3What does "NAT only affects connections that start inside the network" mean?

Devices in an office or a private subnet have private addresses (10.x, 192.168.x) that the internet can't reach. The router does NAT (network address translation):

  1. A laptop opens a connection out.
  2. The router swaps in its own public IP and port, and records the mapping in a table.
  3. A reply arrives on that public port; the router looks it up and hands it back to the laptop.

A connection that starts outside matches no entry, so there's no inside machine to hand it to, and it's dropped.

out:   10.2.129.15:51000  →  NAT  →  52.x.x.x:31744  →  github.com:443   (entry recorded)
reply: github.com:443  →  52.x.x.x:31744  →  table hit  →  10.2.129.15:51000
in:    stranger  →  52.x.x.x:443   →  no entry  →  dropped
In Crucible

People behind NAT reach Crucible fine, because browsers and the laptop agent always connect out to the server. Nothing ever connects in to a laptop.

2.4What is the AWS NAT gateway, and why can't anyone make requests into it?

AWS's managed version of that router, for private subnets. Instances without public IPs reach the internet through it while staying unreachable from outside.

  • Outbound only, by design. Unlike a home router, it offers no port forwarding at all. Instances can fetch updates and call APIs, but nothing on the internet can reach them through it.
  • Cost: roughly $0.045–0.06 an hour plus about $0.045 per GB processed, so over $30 a month even when idle.
  • To accept connections from outside, you need a public IP on the instance or a load balancer in front of it.

2.5What's the difference between a VPC endpoint and an internet gateway?

  • An internet gateway is the VPC's door to the whole public internet. For a server with a public IP, it translates between that public address and the server's private one.
  • A VPC endpoint is a private doorway to one specific destination, without crossing the internet. Three kinds:
    • Gateway endpoints (S3, DynamoDB): a route-table entry that sends that service's traffic over AWS's private network.
    • Interface endpoints (PrivateLink): a network interface with a private IP in your subnet that is the service, e.g. SSM or STS.
    • Gateway Load Balancer endpoints: send traffic into an inspection appliance. AWS Network Firewall uses this kind.
In our account

The spoke subnets' default route points at a vpce-…. That's a Network Firewall endpoint, not a door to the internet.

2.6What is an Elastic IP? Is it a direct bypass to the server?

An Elastic IP (EIP) is a fixed public IPv4 address that the internet gateway maps one-to-one to a server's private address.

It's almost a bypass.

  • What it passes: the internet gateway, which only translates addresses; then the server's security group and the subnet's network ACL.
  • What doesn't happen: nothing reads what the traffic contains. There's no load balancer, no WAF, and normally no central firewall, unless the network team routed inbound traffic through one.

So it doesn't skip every rule, but it skips the controls an organization relies on. That's why SCPs often deny it. The cost is about $3.60 a month, since AWS charges for every public IPv4 address.

2.7What is a security group?

AWS's firewall attached to a network interface: an instance, a load balancer, a VPN endpoint.

  • Allow-only: rules allow traffic by port, protocol and source; anything not allowed is blocked. There are no deny rules.
  • Stateful: if a connection is allowed in, its replies are allowed out automatically.
  • A source can be another security group. "Allow from group X" means "from anything with X attached", which stays right when IPs change.

2.8What is AWS Network Firewall? Is it the same as security groups?

A managed firewall that inspects traffic entering and leaving a VPC.

  • Where it runs: in dedicated firewall subnets, where it appears as VPC endpoints. Route tables steer traffic through it.
  • What it matches: ports and IPs, domain names (e.g. allow only *.github.com), and intrusion-detection signatures.
  • Cost: about $0.40 an hour per endpoint, plus a per-GB charge, usually paid centrally.

It's a separate layer from security groups, and traffic has to pass both:

Security groupNetwork Firewall
WhereOn each network interfaceIn the network path, at the VPC's edge
Who manages itUsually the app teamThe central network or security team
What it can doAllow ports from sources; stateful; no deny rulesAllow and deny; domain names; intrusion signatures; logging

A third, rarely used layer exists: network ACLs, stateless allow and deny lists on whole subnets.

Exposing an internal web app

The conventional front doors, why organizations prefer a load balancer over a public server, and what an ALB with a WAF looks like in our VPC.

In Crucible

Today's deploy is pattern 4 (an Elastic IP on the node), which our SCP blocks. Whatever the pattern:

  • the front door must carry WebSockets (agent link, terminals);
  • its idle timeout should be raised above 60 seconds;
  • TLS moves to the load balancer or proxy;
  • the sign-in callback URL must be the hostname people actually use.

3.2Why is a public load balancer acceptable but a public Elastic IP isn't?

It isn't about having a public IP. It's about what is exposed and who controls it.

  • An Elastic IP on an instance puts the server itself on the internet. Every port its security group allows is open, and that's one edit away from "everything". Traffic skips the central firewall, the WAF and the logging.
  • A load balancer exposes only what it's configured for, typically HTTPS on 443 for named hostnames:
    • it ends TLS and forwards only well-formed HTTP;
    • it can sit behind a WAF, log every request and require sign-in;
    • the servers behind it stay private, so even a wrong security group can't expose SSH.
  • Organizations want one governed front door. If every team could attach Elastic IPs, every team would run its own internet edge.

3.3Does an access proxy log in to AWS with SSO, and is that why it works?

No, it's not an AWS login. It's single sign-on with the company identity provider.

  1. A user opens https://crucible.company.com. That address belongs to the proxy.
  2. The proxy sends them to sign in with the company identity provider, often with MFA, and may check the device is managed.
  3. Only then does it forward the request, over its own private path, to the app. Users need no AWS permissions.

How the proxy reaches the private app:

  • AWS Verified Access connects into your VPC.
  • Cloudflare and Zscaler run a small connector inside your network that dials out to the vendor. That's why it works through NAT.

3.4Why choose pattern 3 at all? Isn't it public access?

Yes, anyone can reach the front door. Teams choose it when the people or systems that need the app can't be on the company network:

  • partners, contractors or external trainees with no VPN account;
  • phones and unmanaged devices;
  • outside services that call in. For Crucible, that's GitHub.com's webhook, and laptop agents at home without a VPN.

It's still much safer than pattern 4. There's one front door, with WAF, rate limits, optional allowlists and sign-in, run by people whose job is the internet edge, and the servers stay private.

In Crucible

If every user is staff, pattern 1 or 2 fits best. Without an inbound path the git webhook can't arrive, so Crucible falls back to fetching every 60 seconds, which is fine.

3.5In pattern 1, why use a load balancer if everyone needs the VPN anyway?

It isn't strictly needed; the VPN only provides reachability. A load balancer adds:

  • TLS certificates from ACM, renewed by AWS. Let's Encrypt's usual check can't reach a private server.
  • A stable address. The node's private IP changes when it's rebuilt; the load balancer's name doesn't.
  • Health checks, two availability zones, access logs, and optionally a WAF.
  • Convention: networking teams often require every app behind one, so all apps are handled alike.

For a single-node app you could skip it. VPN users would reach the node's private IP by an internal DNS name, with TLS from an internal certificate or Let's Encrypt's DNS check. That saves about $16–20 a month.

3.6Can we add an ALB to our network, or does the organization block that too?

Not necessarily blocked; it has to be checked. Two things decide it:

  • Does the SCP allow creating load balancers? The IAM policy simulator can answer that without creating anything, since it includes SCPs in its result.
  • Is there somewhere for it to sit?
    • An internet-facing ALB needs public subnets in two AZs (route to the internet gateway). Here only the networking team's small natSubnets qualify. They're big enough (an ALB needs 8 free addresses per subnet), but using them is the networking team's call.
    • An internal ALB can go in the spoke subnets, and is reachable only from wherever the company network connects in.

The three possible outcomes:

  1. Internet-facing allowed: a public ALB with WAF, if the networking team agrees.
  2. Only internal allowed: pattern 1, reached over the company network or VPN.
  3. Both denied: ingress has to come from the networking team's central entry point.
In our account

Not checked yet: the AWS session expired before the simulator ran. It needs aws login again, then a read-only simulation of an internet-facing and an internal ALB.

3.7How would we set up an ALB with a WAF, and how would the VPC look?

This assumes the organization allows an internet-facing ALB. The internal variant follows below.

What's needed, piece by piece

  1. Public subnets for the ALB in two AZs. Ideally the networking team adds a small dedicated "ingress" subnet in each.
  2. An ACM certificate for crucible.company.com, validated by a DNS record (3.9).
  3. The ALB and its security group:
    • listener :80 redirects to HTTPS;
    • listener :443 uses the certificate and a TLS 1.2/1.3 policy;
    • idle timeout raised from 60 s to about 300 s, for WebSockets (6.3).
  4. A target group with the node on port 80 and a GET /healthz health check (3.8).
  5. A regional AWS WAF web ACL attached to the ALB:
    • AWS managed rules: common exploits, known bad inputs, IP reputation;
    • a rate-based rule;
    • an optional office IP allowlist, which would also block home agents and GitHub unless those paths get exceptions.
  6. DNS: the hostname as an alias or CNAME to the ALB's DNS name.
  7. The node in a private spoke subnet, with no Elastic IP. Its security group allows port 80 only from the ALB's group.
  8. The firewall's outbound allowlist for the node: k3s and GitHub at first boot, Docker registries, the git host, the AWS Price List, and AWS APIs (S3, SSM, STS, Cognito) unless VPC endpoints exist. Let's Encrypt is no longer needed.
In Crucible
  • The WAF's common rule set blocks request bodies over 8 KB. Crucible's uploads and content edits are bigger, so that rule goes to count-only, or excludes those paths. Start the whole WAF in count mode.
  • Traefik stops doing TLS: no Let's Encrypt, no redirect.
  • Rough cost: ALB about $18–25 a month, WAF about $10 a month, ACM free.

If only an internal ALB is allowed

  • The scheme is internal, in the spoke subnets.
  • Users arrive over the VPN or the office network; DNS goes in an internal zone.
  • A WAF is optional (3.10).
  • The GitHub webhook can't arrive, so Crucible polls; home agents need the VPN.

3.8What is a target group?

The ALB's list of where to send traffic, and how to check those destinations are healthy.

  • Members: EC2 instances, IP addresses, or Lambda functions.
  • Port and protocol to forward to on each member, e.g. port 80 over HTTP, since the ALB already handled HTTPS.
  • Health check: the ALB calls a path on each member every few seconds, and only sends traffic to members that pass. If none pass, users get a 503 from the ALB rather than a hang.
  • Other settings: sticky sessions, and a deregistration delay for finishing in-flight requests when a member is removed.

The flow: a listener (:443) receives a request, its rules (by host or path) pick a target group, and the group picks a healthy member. One ALB can serve several apps, each with its own target group.

3.9What is ACM?

AWS Certificate Manager: AWS's service for TLS certificates, the thing behind the padlock in HTTPS.

  • Public certificates are free. You request one and prove you control the domain, usually with a DNS record ACM gives you.
  • It renews them automatically, as long as that DNS record stays.
  • It only works with AWS services: ALB, NLB, CloudFront, API Gateway, Client VPN. AWS holds the private key, and by default you can't export it.
  • ACM Private CA is a separate, paid internal certificate authority. A public certificate on an internal ALB is usually simpler, and works even when the site is only reachable over a VPN.
In Crucible

Behind an ALB, ACM replaces Let's Encrypt, which needs the internet to reach port 80.

3.10If we set up a VPN, do we need a WAF? What are the conventions?

Not strictly. A WAF mainly defends apps the internet can reach.

  • Internet-facing apps: a WAF is standard, often required. Anyone can send traffic, so you want attack filtering, IP reputation, rate limits and bot control.
  • Internal apps behind a VPN: usually no WAF. The defense is that only company people and devices can reach the app, plus its own login. A WAF there mostly adds false positives and cost.
  • The exception: zero-trust or compliance-driven companies. Some treat the internal network as untrusted too, since a compromised laptop is on the VPN as well. Some audits (PCI DSS, some SOC 2 controls) expect a WAF in front of web apps with sensitive data. AWS WAF works on internal ALBs too.

Behind a VPN, these still matter:

  • TLS with ACM, even inside the network;
  • every user signs in to the app;
  • narrow security groups (ALB from VPN and office ranges, node from the ALB only);
  • ALB access logs and CloudTrail;
  • patching.
In Crucible
  • A WAF can't see inside WebSockets: it inspects the upgrade request and nothing after the 101.
  • Crucible already checks Origin on every state change, sends a strict CSP, checks permissions per route and checks the webhook secret.
  • Recommendation: start without a WAF behind a VPN, and add one, in count mode first, if Crucible is ever exposed publicly.

CHAPTER 04

Connecting offices and people

How separate networks join AWS: the Transit Gateway hub, site-to-site VPNs and private lines, and VPNs for individual people.

4.1What is a Transit Gateway?

An AWS-managed router for a whole region. It joins many VPCs, plus offices and data centers (over VPN or Direct Connect), as a hub. Without it, every pair of VPCs would need its own peering link.

  • Attachments are its ports: one per VPC, VPN or Direct Connect gateway.
  • Its route tables are the rulebook, e.g. "office A may reach spokes 1 to 5" and "spokes may not reach each other".
  • In large organizations, every account's spoke VPC attaches to it, often with a central inspection VPC in the path.

4.2How does an office network connect to a Transit Gateway and a VPC, when the office is a separate building?

With an encrypted tunnel over the internet, or a private fiber line. Either way, private addresses on both sides keep working as if they were one network.

1. Site-to-Site VPN, the most common

  • The office side is a router or firewall the company already has, with a public IP.
  • The AWS side is a customer gateway resource recording that IP, plus a VPN connection attached to the Transit Gateway. AWS always provides two tunnels, in different AZs.
  • Routing: the office advertises its ranges and AWS advertises the VPC ranges, usually with BGP.
  • Return route: the spoke subnets need a route back to the office range through the Transit Gateway.
  • Speed and cost: up to about 1.25 Gbps per tunnel, roughly $0.05 an hour per connection plus data.

2. AWS Direct Connect, a private line

  • A physical fiber connection in a shared data center, between the company's router (or a telecom partner's) and AWS. Traffic never crosses the internet.
  • A "transit" virtual interface leads to a Direct Connect gateway, then to the Transit Gateway.
  • It takes weeks to set up and costs more. A VPN often runs on top for encryption, or as a backup.

3. People at home

They use the company's remote-access VPN, which ends at the office or a cloud VPN service, then follows the same path into AWS. The alternative is AWS Client VPN straight into AWS.

Hard rule: IP ranges must not overlap. That's why central teams hand each spoke its own range.

4.3How exactly is a VPN connected to a Transit Gateway?

As an attachment. The two kinds of VPN plug in differently.

Site-to-Site VPN

  1. Describe the office: a customer gateway with the router's public IP and its BGP AS number. 
    aws ec2 create-customer-gateway --type ipsec.1 --public-ip 203.0.113.10 --bgp-asn 65010
  2. Create the VPN connection aimed at the Transit Gateway. That creates the VPN attachment automatically. (Aiming it at a "virtual private gateway" instead connects one VPC only, the older way.) 
    aws ec2 create-vpn-connection --type ipsec.1 \
  --customer-gateway-id cgw-… --transit-gateway-id tgw-…
  3. AWS provisions two tunnels. Each has an outside public IP in a different AZ, inside link addresses (e.g. 169.254.10.0/30) for routing, and a pre-shared key. AWS generates a config file for the router's brand.
  4. The router brings the tunnels up. IKE authenticates both ends and agrees on keys; IPsec then encrypts and wraps every packet.
  5. BGP exchanges routes inside each tunnel. If one tunnel drops, traffic moves to the other within seconds. With BGP the Transit Gateway can also use both tunnels at once (ECMP).
  6. Transit Gateway route tables decide who sees whom. Each attachment is associated with one table, used for traffic entering from it. Routes arrive by propagation from attachments, or as static entries.
  7. The VPC needs a route back: 192.168.0.0/16 → tgw-… in the spoke subnets' route table, or replies leave through the firewall and NAT and get lost.
laptop 192.168.1.20 → office router (10.2.128.0/22 via tunnel)
  → encrypted over the internet → TGW VPN attachment (decrypted)
  → attachment's route table: 10.2.128.0/22 → our VPC attachment → internal ALB
reply: VPC route 192.168.0.0/16 → TGW → VPN attachment → tunnel → office

Client VPN

It doesn't attach to a Transit Gateway directly.

  • Its endpoint is associated with subnets in one VPC, usually a central networking VPC that has a Transit Gateway attachment.
  • You add routes and authorization rules for each range users should reach.
  • The path: laptop → Client VPN endpoint → that VPC → Transit Gateway → our spoke.

4.4How do we set up a VPN into the internal subnet?

First ask whether one exists. In a spoke-and-hub organization, the company VPN usually already reaches the spokes through the Transit Gateway. Then it's a route and a firewall rule for 10.2.128.0/22, done by the networking team.

If you need your own, from smallest to largest:

  1. SSM port forwarding, just for admins and testing (1.9). No VPN at all.
  2. AWS Client VPN, managed OpenVPN for individual people:
    • a server certificate in ACM;
    • a client address range that overlaps nothing, e.g. 10.200.0.0/22;
    • sign-in through SAML (company login with MFA);
    • split tunnel on, so only VPC traffic uses the VPN;
    • the VPC resolver for DNS;
    • associated with both spoke subnets, with an authorization rule for 10.2.128.0/22;
    • its own security group, allowed into the internal ALB.
    It costs about $73 a month per associated subnet, plus about $0.05 an hour per connected user. Check the SCP first: ec2:CreateClientVpnEndpoint may be denied.
  3. Site-to-Site VPN for a whole office (4.2). Network-team territory.
  4. Zero-trust alternatives: Tailscale or Cloudflare Tunnel (chapter 5). Light and cheap, but they bypass the company's network design, so they need security approval.
In Crucible
  • Browsers and terminals work normally behind a VPN.
  • Laptop agents connect only while the VPN is up.
  • Cluster labs are unaffected, since they reach the trainee's own cluster.
  • The GitHub webhook can't arrive, so Crucible polls every 60 seconds.
CHAPTER 05

Zero-trust access

Two ways into a closed network that need nothing inbound: a mesh VPN and an outbound tunnel with sign-in at the edge.

5.1How do Tailscale and Cloudflare Tunnel work?

Both work through NAT without opening anything inbound, but at different layers. Tailscale is a VPN: it gives devices network access to private IPs. Cloudflare Tunnel is a reverse proxy: it gives browsers access to specific web apps.

Tailscale

  • What it is: a mesh VPN built on WireGuard. Each signed-in device joins your "tailnet" with a stable 100.x.y.z address.
  • Control plane: Tailscale's servers. Devices sign in through your identity provider and receive keys and access rules there. Your traffic never passes through it.
  • Data plane: devices talk directly, encrypted end to end. Both sides connect out and "punch through" NAT. If that fails, encrypted packets go via Tailscale's DERP relays on 443, which can't read them.
  • Access rules: a policy file, e.g. "group:trainees may reach tag:crucible on 443". Everything else is denied.
  • Putting Crucible on it:
    • install Tailscale on the node (tailscale serve can even provide HTTPS);
    • or run a subnet router in the spoke that advertises 10.2.128.0/22.
    Keep tailscale funnel off: it publishes a service to the internet.

Cloudflare Tunnel with Access

  1. cloudflared runs inside your network and connects out to several Cloudflare data centers, keeping those connections open. It uses port 7844, falling back to 443.
  2. DNS: the hostname points to Cloudflare, so the domain or subdomain must be on Cloudflare.
  3. Access: Cloudflare Access makes users sign in with the company identity provider, checks your rules, and sets a session cookie.
  4. Requests: allowed requests travel down the already-open tunnel to cloudflared, which hands them to the app locally. WebSockets work.

CHAPTER 06

Long-lived web connections

HTTP keep-alive, WebSockets, and why idle timeouts on load balancers matter for Crucible's terminals and agents.

6.1What is HTTP keep-alive, and how does it work?

The problem it solves: each new HTTPS connection costs round trips before any data moves, about one for TCP and one or two for TLS. On a 50 ms link that's 100–150 ms of waiting. HTTP 1.0 closed the connection after every response, so every image and API call paid that again.

Keep-alive means: after the response, leave the connection open and reuse it.

  • HTTP/1.1 connections are persistent by default; either side ends one with Connection: close.
  • Idle connections stay open for a while, often 5–60 seconds, sometimes announced as Keep-Alive: timeout=5, max=100.
  • Requests take turns on one HTTP/1.1 connection, so a slow response blocks the ones behind it (head-of-line blocking). That's why browsers open about six connections per site.
  • HTTP/2 and HTTP/3 carry many requests at once on one connection, as interleaved streams.

The rule never changes: the client asks, the server answers. Keep-alive only saves setup time.

6.2Is a WebSocket basically a persistent HTTP connection? How does it differ from keep-alive?

Close, but after the first exchange it stops being HTTP.

  1. The client sends an ordinary HTTP request with Upgrade: websocket and a random Sec-WebSocket-Key.
  2. The server answers 101 Switching Protocols, with a value derived from that key.
  3. The same connection now carries small frames: text, binary, ping/pong and close.
  4. Either side can send at any moment, both at once (full duplex), for hours.
HTTP with keep-aliveWebSocket
PurposeReuse a connection to save setup timeHold an open two-way channel
Who can sendClient asks, server answersEither side, any time
UnitRequest and response, each with headersSmall frames, a few bytes of overhead
Server pushing newsImpossible; the client pollsNatural

Ping/pong is the WebSocket's own keep-alive, unrelated to HTTP keep-alive despite the name. It stops NATs, proxies and load balancers from deciding a quiet connection is dead.

In Crucible
  • Ordinary API calls are plain HTTP.
  • The laptop agent link (/api/agent/ws) and browser terminals are WebSockets.
  • Each agent's connection lives in one process's memory, which is why Crucible runs one API replica.

6.3Do WebSockets follow the HTTP API call timeouts?

No. After the upgrade a WebSocket isn't a request any more, so per-request timeouts stop applying. What counts instead:

  • Handshake timeouts: the upgrade itself is still HTTP. Crucible gives clients 10 seconds to send headers, and sets no overall read or write timeout that would cut a long connection.
  • Idle timeouts at every hop:
    • AWS ALB: 60 seconds by default;
    • Cloudflare: about 100 seconds;
    • corporate proxies, NAT gateways and home routers: minutes to hours.
    Any frame resets the clock, including a ping.
  • The app's own limits: Crucible bounds each message write, not the connection's lifetime.
In Crucible
  • Agent link: the server pings every 15 seconds and drops the connection after 10 seconds without a reply. That stays well inside 60 seconds, and the agent reconnects by itself.
  • Browser terminals have no server ping. A terminal quiet for over 60 seconds would be cut by an ALB with default settings. So raise the idle timeout to about 300 seconds; the sturdier fix is a server-side ping, a small change.
CHAPTER 07

Kubernetes networking

How a request travels from a node's port to a pod, which pieces rewrite packets and which are real proxies, and how the pod network itself works.

7.1What is Traefik, and why does Crucible's cluster need it?

Traefik isn't an AWS service. It's an open-source reverse proxy and Kubernetes ingress controller, built into k3s. On Crucible's AWS node it's the front door: the only thing on the node's ports 80 and 443, handing requests to the Crucible pod.

  • The routing rule: the Helm chart's Ingress says "host crucible.company.com, HTTPS, certificate from resolver le → service crucible port 80".
  • The configuration: bootstrap.sh.tftpl writes a k3s HelmChartConfig with:
    • a Let's Encrypt resolver using the HTTP-01 check on port 80;
    • a small volume for acme.json, so restarts don't request new certificates;
    • the HTTP → HTTPS redirect.

Why it's needed:

  • TLS: Crucible speaks plain HTTP on 8080, and browsers need HTTPS for secure cookies, sign-in callbacks and wss:// terminals.
  • Reaching a pod: pods don't listen on the node's ports by themselves; an ingress controller bridges that.
  • Cost: it comes with k3s, so there's no ALB and no ACM certificate.

What it doesn't do: no sign-in, no WAF, and no real load balancing with one replica. Locally there's no Traefik at all.

In Crucible

With an ALB or a Cloudflare Tunnel in front, Traefik stays as the router inside the cluster, but its certificate job moves to the front door.

7.2How does Traefik load-balance across multiple replicas?

Traefik skips the Service's virtual IP. It watches the Service's EndpointSlices, the list of ready pod IPs, and connects to pods directly.

  • Algorithm: weighted round robin, with equal weights by default.
  • Health: only pods passing their readiness probe appear in the list. A failing pod drops out within a second or two.
  • Sticky sessions (optional): the annotation traefik.ingress.kubernetes.io/service.sticky.cookie: "true" pins a browser to one pod with a cookie.
  • WebSockets are balanced when they open, and stay on that pod until they close.
  • Updates to the list apply live, with no reload.
In Crucible

Crucible is pinned to replicas: 1 with the Recreate strategy on purpose. The agent hub and locks live in memory, so an agent's WebSocket and its trainee's browser could land on different pods. A sticky cookie wouldn't fix that, because they're separate clients. More replicas would need shared state, which is an app change.

7.3Can Traefik load-balance across multiple nodes? Is "a load balancer in front, Traefik on each node" right?

Yes, in both senses.

  • To pods on other nodes: automatic. The pod network spans all nodes, so Traefik on node 1 reaches a pod on node 3 by its IP.
  • As an entry point spread over nodes: something in front has to pick a node and notice a dead one. Traefik can't do that for itself. That's the standard pattern you described.

7.4What is a DaemonSet?

A Kubernetes object that runs exactly one copy of a pod on every node, or on each node matching a selector.

  • No replica count: the number of copies is the number of nodes.
  • Limiting it: a nodeSelector or affinity restricts it to some nodes.
  • Used for things that belong to a machine:
    • ingress at the node's edge;
    • log collectors (Fluent Bit);

    • storage drivers (CSI node plugins).

In k3s, ServiceLB's svclb-traefik-… pods are a DaemonSet. Traefik itself is a one-replica Deployment.

7.5What is klipper-lb, how does it bind ports, and is it a proxy?

klipper-lb is k3s's built-in implementation of type: LoadBalancer Services. k3s calls it ServiceLB; klipper-lb is the small container it runs. On a plain machine nothing would create a cloud load balancer, so it uses the nodes' own IPs instead.

  1. k3s creates the Service traefik, of type LoadBalancer, on ports 80 and 443.
  2. ServiceLB creates a DaemonSet svclb-traefik-…, so one pod runs on every node.
  3. Each pod declares hostPort: 80 and 443.
  4. ServiceLB writes the node IPs into the Service's status, which is why kubectl get svc shows an "external IP".

It isn't a proxy; it does NAT.

  • A proxy (Traefik, an ALB) ends your connection and opens a new one, reading every byte.
  • klipper-lb only rewrites packet addresses and lets the kernel forward them, so it stays one connection and no program touches the data.

The container runs a short script once, then sleeps:

echo 1 > /proc/sys/net/ipv4/ip_forward                       # allow forwarding
iptables -t nat -A PREROUTING  -p tcp --dport 443 \
         -j DNAT --to-destination <traefik ClusterIP>:443     # rewrite destination
iptables -t nat -A POSTROUTING -j MASQUERADE                 # replies come back this way
sleep forever

Even hostPort isn't a real socket: the CNI's portmap plugin writes a redirect rule on the node.

Consequences:

  • one owner per port per node;
  • no spreading across nodes and no failover;
  • turn it off with --disable servicelb when an ALB or MetalLB takes over.

7.6On EKS, how does a LoadBalancer Service create a real load balancer? And what are NodePorts?

Kubernetes doesn't make load balancers itself. A controller watches for type: LoadBalancer Services and calls the cloud's API. On EKS that's the AWS Load Balancer Controller, or the older built-in cloud provider.

  1. You apply a LoadBalancer Service.
  2. The controller calls AWS to create an NLB, a target group and listeners.
  3. It registers targets: the nodes, or the pod IPs.
  4. It writes the NLB's DNS name into the Service's status.
  • Deleting the Service deletes the NLB.
  • Annotations set options, e.g. service.beta.kubernetes.io/aws-load-balancer-scheme: internal.
  • An Ingress with the same controller makes an ALB instead.

klipper-lb is k3s's stand-in for that controller.

NodePort

A Service type that opens the same port, in the range 30000–32767, on every node. Traffic to any node on that port reaches the Service's pods through kube-proxy.

ALB → target group: node1:30080, node2:30080, node3:30080
        → kube-proxy rules on that node → Traefik pod (any node) → app pod

Why the ALB targets NodePorts: it can only send to addresses the VPC can route. On k3s, pod IPs come from flannel's private overlay (7.10), which the VPC can't route, but node IPs are real VPC addresses. That's instance mode. IP mode, targeting pod IPs directly, needs pods with real VPC addresses, as the AWS VPC CNI gives them on EKS (7.11).

7.7Do we need a Traefik pod on every node? What's the convention?

Technically one is enough: every node's svclb pod forwards to the Traefik Service, wherever the one pod runs. The catches:

  • Single point of failure: if that pod or its node dies, web traffic stops until it's rescheduled, about a minute.
  • Extra hops: a request can cross nodes to reach Traefik, then cross again to the app.
  • Lost client IP: forwarding across nodes rewrites the source. externalTrafficPolicy: Local keeps the real IP, but then only nodes with a Traefik pod accept traffic.

7.8What is kube-proxy, and how does it work?

A small program on every node that makes Service addresses work. In most clusters it's a DaemonSet; in k3s it's built into the k3s process.

The problem: a Service's ClusterIP, e.g. 10.43.0.50, belongs to no machine, and nothing listens there.

  1. It watches Services and their EndpointSlices.
  2. It writes kernel rules, usually iptables, or nftables or IPVS.
  3. The kernel does the forwarding. kube-proxy isn't in the traffic path, despite its name.
  4. Connection tracking (conntrack) keeps each connection on the pod it first picked, which matters for long WebSockets.
packet to 10.43.0.50:80 (ClusterIP) or node:30080 (NodePort)
  → 1/3 → 10.42.1.11:8080
    1/2 → 10.42.2.12:8080
    else → 10.42.3.13:8080
  → rewrite destination (DNAT), send on

Cilium can replace kube-proxy, doing the same job with eBPF programs.

7.9Is MetalLB the same as klipper-lb?

Same job, implementing LoadBalancer Services without a cloud, but a different mechanism.

MetalLB's two modes:

  • L2 mode: one node answers "that IP is me" (ARP), and another takes over if it dies. Simple, but one node carries all of that IP's traffic.
  • BGP mode: every node advertises the IP to the routers, which spread traffic across nodes. Real load balancing, but it needs BGP-capable routers.

On AWS, neither fits well. The VPC ignores "that IP is me" announcements, because an IP only works when AWS assigned it to an interface, and the VPC router doesn't accept BGP from your nodes. On AWS the job goes to an ALB or NLB.

7.10What is anti-affinity? What is a PodDisruptionBudget?

Anti-affinity is a scheduling rule: "don't put this pod on the same node (or zone) as pods labelled X". It spreads replicas so one failure can't take them all.

affinity:
  podAntiAffinity:
    requiredDuringSchedulingIgnoredDuringExecution:   # hard; "preferred…" is soft
      - labelSelector: { matchLabels: { app: traefik } }
        topologyKey: kubernetes.io/hostname           # same node; …/zone for same zone
  • Hard vs soft: with the hard rule, a third replica on two nodes stays Pending; the soft one places it anyway.
  • Affinity is the opposite: "put me near pods X".
  • topologySpreadConstraints is the newer way to say "spread evenly".

A PodDisruptionBudget (PDB) says "during planned maintenance, never take down more than this many of my pods at once".

apiVersion: policy/v1
kind: PodDisruptionBudget
metadata: { name: traefik }
spec:
  minAvailable: 1          # or maxUnavailable: 1
  selector: { matchLabels: { app: traefik } }
  • It guards voluntary disruptions that use the eviction API: kubectl drain, cluster upgrades, the autoscaler removing a node. Evictions that would break the budget are refused and retried.
  • It doesn't guard crashes, a dying node, or kubectl delete pod.
  • The trap: with one replica and minAvailable: 1, every drain blocks forever. That's why Crucible, with one replica by design, has no PDB.

7.11What is a CNI, and how does Flannel's VXLAN work?

CNI (Container Network Interface) is a standard for how a container gets its network: a short spec plus plugin programs that follow it.

What Kubernetes requires of the network:

  • every pod gets its own IP;
  • every pod reaches every other pod, on any node, without address rewriting;
  • nodes reach pods.

When a pod starts:

  1. containerd creates an empty network namespace for it.
  2. It runs the plugin (in /opt/cni/bin, configured in /etc/cni/net.d/) with ADD.
  3. The plugin creates a veth pair, a virtual cable with one end in the pod as eth0. It assigns an IP (IPAM) and sets routes.
  4. DEL cleans up when the pod is deleted.

Plugins can be chained; portmap, which implements hostPort, runs after the main one.

PluginHow pods on different nodes talk
Flannel (k3s default)Overlay: wraps pod packets inside node packets (VXLAN)
CalicoUsually plain routes or BGP; optional overlay; network policies
CiliumeBPF; overlay or native routing; can replace kube-proxy
AWS VPC CNINo wrapping: pods get real VPC IPs (7.12)

Flannel VXLAN, step by step

  1. Address plan: pods share 10.42.0.0/16, and each node gets a /24 slice: node 1 10.42.0.0/24, node 2 10.42.1.0/24.
  2. Devices on each node: cni0, a virtual bridge every pod plugs into, and flannel.1, the VXLAN device that wraps and unwraps.
  3. flanneld writes, for each other node:
    • a route: "10.42.1.0/24 via flannel.1";
    • that node's tunnel MAC address;
    • "that MAC lives at 10.2.128.11".
  4. The kernel does all the wrapping. flanneld isn't in the traffic path.

Practical consequences:

  • each packet grows by 50 bytes, so pod interfaces get a smaller MTU (e.g. 1450 instead of 1500);
  • node security groups must allow UDP 8472 between nodes;
  • Flannel's other modes: host-gw (plain routes, needs one flat network) and wireguard-native (encrypted).

On Crucible's single node, nothing ever goes through VXLAN.

7.12What is the AWS VPC CNI?

EKS's default CNI (the aws-node DaemonSet). Instead of an overlay, it gives each pod a real VPC address from the subnet, as a secondary IP on the node's network interfaces (ENIs). It keeps spare IPs ready and attaches extra ENIs as pods arrive.

Flannel (k3s)                              VPC CNI (EKS)
node 10.2.128.10                           node 10.2.128.10
  └ pod 10.42.0.5  ← VPC can't route here     ├ pod 10.2.128.31  ← a real VPC address
                                              └ pod 10.2.128.32

What it makes possible:

  • ALB IP mode, with no NodePort hop;
  • security groups per pod;
  • flow logs and firewalls that see pod addresses;
  • no wrapping overhead.

What it costs:

  • It uses subnet IPs fast: every pod takes one, and a /22 has about 1,000 for everything.
  • The instance size caps pods: a t3.medium allows 3 ENIs × 6 IPs, about 17 pods.
  • The fixes: prefix delegation, which hands out a /28 per slot, and custom networking, which puts pods in a secondary range such as 100.64.0.0/16.
In our account

Our spoke is a /22. If Crucible ever moved to EKS, plan prefix delegation or a secondary range with the networking team first.

CHAPTER 08

Signing people in

Cognito, Google sign-in and Keycloak for the app itself.

8.1Does Cognito cost money?

Crucible's user pool uses the Essentials tier, for the modern login pages. Per docs/runbooks/cognito.md, it's free up to 10,000 monthly active users, then about a cent per user. A training platform will almost certainly stay free. Check AWS's Cognito pricing page for current rates.

8.2Can sign-in use Google, through Cognito or through Keycloak?

  • Cognito: yes. Add Google as a social identity provider with OAuth credentials from Google Cloud Console. One catch: Cognito can't limit Google sign-in to the company domain by itself. A small "pre sign-up" Lambda that rejects other domains does that.
  • Keycloak: yes, with domain limits built in. Google is a built-in identity provider. The trade-off is that production doesn't run Keycloak; you'd host it yourself, with its own database, backups and upgrades.
In Crucible

Crucible is a standard OIDC client, so the choice is just an issuer and a client id. The recommendation is Google in Cognito plus the domain-limiting Lambda. If the company already connects its identity provider to IAM Identity Center, Cognito → Identity Center (1.2) is the other route. Neither is built yet.
